#### **1. Introduction
Naturally occurring radioactive materials (NORM), notably radionuclides from the uranium-238 (U-238) and thorium-232 (Th-232) decay series, as well as potassium-40 (K-40), are ubiquitous in geological raw materials (UNSCEAR, 2000; Rodrigues e Silva and Monteiro de Oliveira, 2023). When such materials (e.g., aggregates, sand, cement, concrete, natural stones, recycled building materials) are used in construction, they can contribute to indoor gamma radiation exposure and, in certain cases, a non-negligible contribution to indoor radon concentration, as construction materials rich in Ra-226 can release radon gas into the indoor environment, thus potentially increasing long-term radiological dose to building occupants. Several studies across Europe and beyond have documented this phenomenon, including comprehensive reviews of the radioactivity of building materials and their contribution to indoor exposure (Anagnostakis et al., 2004; Beretka and Mathew, 1985; Trevisi et al., 2012; Papastefanou et al., 2005). 

Large-scale surveys across the European Union have documented wide variability in NORM activity concentrations in building materials and associated exposure scenarios (UNSCEAR, 2008), while national studies in Ireland remain limited (Lee et al., 2004). In addition, coordinated European initiatives such as the e-NORM survey have assessed exposure situations involving naturally occurring radioactive materials across multiple EU Member States, highlighting substantial differences in exposure scenarios, regulatory approaches, and the radiological relevance of NORM between countries (Mrdakovic Popic et al., 2023). More recently, a systematic methodology for the identification and prioritisation of NORM has been proposed at the European level, providing a harmonised framework to support regulatory decisionmaking and risk-based screening approaches (Michalik et al., 2023; Djabou et al., 2026). Complementary databases comprising thousands of samples of bulk and superficial construction materials further demonstrate the wide range of Ra-226, Th-232, and K-40 activity concentrations observed across Europe (Madruga et al., 2019; Trevisi et al., 2012). 

To address this gap, the NORM-BMI project was initially undertaken as a baseline assessment of NORM activity in commonly used and recycled building materials. Activity concentrations were measured and compared across three laboratories: The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA, Ireland), University College Dublin (UCD, Ireland), and the University of Cantabria (UC, Spain), providing preliminary insights into radionuclide variability and measurement reproducibility, and highlighting the need for more comprehensive, systematic evaluations in subsequent project phases. 

Building on these findings, this study, conducted in two phases, aims to expand the dataset of materials widely used in Irish construction and to evaluate measurement consistency across laboratories. Identical samples were analysed independently by the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), University College Dublin (UCD), and University of Cantabria (UC) laboratories. Ensuring inter-laboratory comparability is essential for reliable national monitoring procedures and evidencebased regulatory guidance. 

Materials selected for the first phase included two types of aggregates 

from suppliers, construction sands, and concrete samples collected from residential demolition sites dating back to 1964. These materials were chosen not only because of their widespread use in Irish construction and relevance to long-term indoor exposure scenarios, but also to investigate the potential for safely recycling demolition-derived building materials. This approach allows assessment of whether the reuse of such materials could contribute to sustainability goals by reducing landfill waste, while ensuring that Gamma Index values and radiological safety remain within acceptable limits. In total, eight unique samples were analysed, with each sample measured sequentially by the EPA, UCD, and UC laboratories, resulting in 24 individual measurements. 

This study reports activity concentrations of radionuclides from the Ra-226 and Th-232 decay series, as well as K-40, together with the corresponding Gamma Index (Iγ) for each material type. The results provide an expanded understanding of NORM variability in Irish building materials and validate the robustness and reproducibility of inter-laboratory measurements within the NORM-BMI project. 

The objectives of the second phase were threefold: (i) to investigate the influence of particle size (grain size fraction) on radionuclide activity concentrations in concrete and aggregate; (ii) to assess the variability of NORM activity associated with high- and low-activity aggregate sources, as identified from national geological (Tellus) data (Geological Survey Ireland, 2014); and (iii) to evaluate cementitious additives, including ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS) and fly ash, due to their increasing use in low-carbon construction and their potential to contain elevated NORM levels. Twelve representative samples were analysed, including concrete, aggregates, cementitious additives, and imported ceramic tiles commonly used in Ireland. Unlike the first phase, samples in this phase were measured independently by a single laboratory (EPA, UCD, or UC) using harmonised measurement protocols, reflecting the more targeted scope of this phase. 

In addition, the inclusion of materials recovered from residential demolition sites in the first phase provides valuable evidence for assessing the radiological safety of recycled construction materials. As the reuse and recycling of demolition waste are increasingly promoted within European circular-economy and sustainability strategies, understanding the NORM content of such materials is essential for their safe incorporation into new construction. By examining particle-size effects, aggregate source variability, and cementitious additives, the second phase provides essential data to support evidence-based assessment of radiological risk in Irish construction materials. The results also contribute to the safe and sustainable use of building materials, inform potential regulatory guidance, enhance national understanding of NORM distribution, and demonstrate the potential for recycling demolition-derived materials, supporting European initiatives for waste reduction, circular economy, and a greener built environment.

#### _2.1. Material selection_ 

The materials were selected to represent the most commonly used

#### _2.1.1. First phase_ 

Materials for this phase were selected to achieve two main objectives: to benchmark the performance of the three participating laboratories (EPA, UCD, UC) on identical samples, and to expand the dataset of NORM concentrations in building materials commonly used in Ireland. Selection criteria combined scientific relevance, representativeness, and logistical feasibility. Priority was given to materials frequently used in the Irish construction sector, including aggregates, construction sands, and concrete types from both demolition and new construction sources. Practical considerations, such as sample availability and representativeness of typical construction practices, also guided the selection process. 

The samples for this phase comprised two aggregate samples sourced from major commercial quarries in Ireland, construction sands from two suppliers, and three concrete and plaster samples obtained from residential demolition sites dating back to 1964. Additional concrete samples were included to reflect historical variations in construction practices. In total, eight samples were selected and shipped sequentially to the three laboratories (EPA → UCD → UC) in sealed 500 mL containers to minimise potential loss or contamination and ensure comparability of measurements. 

These materials provide a diverse representation of Irish building products, encompassing raw aggregates, finished construction materials, and demolition-derived samples. This selection allowed for a comprehensive assessment of Ra-226, Th-232, and K-40 activity concentrations and the corresponding Gamma Index (Iγ). The resulting dataset supports both validation of laboratory performance and expansion of national NORM screening data to inform regulatory frameworks and public health decision-making. 

#### _2.1.2. Second phase_ 

Materials for the second phase were selected to achieve main objectives: to investigate the influence of particle size on radionuclide activity concentrations in concrete; to assess the variability of NORM activity associated with high- and low-activity aggregate sources identified from national geological (Tellus) data; to evaluate cementitious additives, including GGBS and fly ash, due to their increasing use in lowcarbon construction and potential for elevated NORM levels; and to assess the contribution of imported construction products commonly used in Ireland. 

The sample set for this phase comprised 12 representative materials. Concrete samples were separated into three grain size fractions ( _<_ 1.18 mm, 1.18–2 mm, and _>_ 2 mm), In addition, one aggregate sample was subdivided into two particle size fractions (approximately 1 mm and 1 cm) to assess particle-size effects on radionuclide activity concentrations. Aggregates were sourced from regional quarries with predicted high- and low-NORM activity levels. Cementitious additives included GGBS and two fly ash samples obtained from power stations. Two imported ceramic tiles from Spain and Italy were also included to evaluate the potential contribution of imported construction materials to indoor NORM exposure. 

Unlike the first phase, each sample in this phase was measured only once in each participating laboratory (EPA, UCD and UC), reflecting the streamlined scope of the study while still providing robust data for comparative assessment. 

#### _2.2. Sample preparation_ 

For all phases of this study, samples were prepared using harmonised procedures to ensure uniformity and comparability across laboratories. 

Initially, all samples were manually cleaned to remove surface dust, coatings, and any adhering organic material. The materials were then mechanically reduced to coarse fragments of approximately 3 cm in size using a combination of hammers, chisels, and concrete compression equipment. 

The coarse fragments were subsequently ground into fine powder, achieving a particle size of less than 1 mm. This fine homogenisation is in line with IAEA recommendations for gamma spectrometric analysis (IAEA, 2010) and ensures consistent measurement conditions across laboratories. After milling, the powders were sieved to obtain uniform particle size and transferred into sealed containers for shipment. 

Upon arrival at the EPA, UCD, and UC laboratories, aliquots from each sample were prepared in counting geometries appropriate for the detector geometry calibration specific to each laboratory. All samples were sealed and stored for a minimum of 28 days prior to measurement to allow secular equilibrium to establish between Ra-226 and its shortlived progeny Pb-214 and Bi-214 via Rn-222 (half-life 3.82 days). This waiting period, widely adopted in gamma spectrometry protocols, ensures that Ra-226 activity can be accurately inferred from its decay products, following established gamma spectrometry protocols and ISO 18589-2 guidelines for environmental radioactivity measurements (ISO, 2015; IAEA, 2010). 

#### _2.3. Gamma spectrometry measurements_ 

Gamma spectrometry analyses for first phase were conducted at the three participating laboratories using high-purity germanium (HPGe) detectors. For the second phase, the same protocols were applied, but analyses were carried out only at the UCD and EPA laboratories. Each laboratory employed independent systems and calibration protocols to ensure inter-laboratory comparability. 

At the University of Cantabria (UC), measurements were performed using a planar lithium-drifted HPGe detector (GeLi HP, Canberra Model 7229-3020) with a relative efficiency of 20% at the 1332 keV gamma line of Co-60. The detector demonstrated good performance across the energy range, with typical absolute efficiencies of 5% at 59.5 keV (Am241), 0.9% at 662 keV (Cs-137), and 0.4% at 1460 keV (K-40). Energy resolution was _<_ 1%, corresponding to 0.45 keV at 59.5 keV and 1.90 keV at 1332 keV, enabling reliable separation of closely spaced gamma lines. 

The detector operated in a low-background environment and was calibrated for both energy and absolute full-energy peak efficiency using certified multi-gamma reference sources covering the energy range of interest (30–3000 keV). Efficiency calibration was performed using the same geometry as the samples (250 mL containers) to ensure consistency in efficiency determination. The calibration was verified periodically to ensure stability and accuracy of the measurements. 

Gamma energies from 30 to 3000 keV were recorded, with samples placed in 250 mL geometry containers for 2 h/measurement. Corrections for true coincidence summing and self-attenuation were applied using Genie 2000 software (Canberra Industries, 2000), incorporating known nuclear decay schemes and sample composition data (Quindos ´ et al., 2006). At the EPA Laboratory, supplementary measurements were carried out using a planar HPGe detector (ORTEC GMX-7023-S) with a relative efficiency of 70% at 1.33 MeV (Co-60) and high energy resolution (FWHM of 122 eV at 14.4 keV and 1.22 keV at 1332 keV). Samples were counted in 250 mL cylindrical containers for 24 h following a minimum 28-day storage period to allow secular equilibrium between Ra-226 and its progeny. Efficiency calibration employed certified multi-gamma reference standards. Spectrum acquisition and analysis were conducted with Genie 2000 and Apex-Gamma modules. Corrections for true coincidence summing and self-attenuation were performed using GESPECOR software (Sima et al., 2001). 

At University College Dublin (UCD), measurements utilised an HPGe detector (Ortec GMX-15190-SV, relative efficiency 19% at 1.33 MeV) coupled with an Ortec DSPEC 50 digital signal processor. Samples were

The activity concentration A(E) of radionuclides in the study samples, expressed in Becquerels per kilogram (Bq⋅kg<sup>−1</sup> ), was calculated using the equation (Knoll, 2010; Gilmore, 2008): 

where Cnet is the net count rate of a specific gamma line after background subtraction, ε(E) is the full-energy peak efficiency at energy E, I is the gamma emission probability, t is the measurement time in seconds, and m is the sample mass in kilograms (Djabou et al., 2022; Quindos et al., 2006´ ). 

Ra-226 and Th-232 activities were determined indirectly through the gamma emissions of their decay products, assuming secular equilibrium within their respective decay chains. For Ra-226, gamma lines from Pb214 at 295.1 keV (19.2%) and 351.9 keV (37.1%), as well as from Bi-214 at 1764.5 keV (15.4%), were primarily used at UC to minimise true coincidence summing effects. UCD additionally employed the 609.3 keV gamma line of Bi-214 (45.5%) due to its strong intensity and clear spectral presence. At the EPA, multiple Bi-214 lines (609, 1120, and 1764 keV) were included for Ra-226 assessment. 

For Th-232, gamma emissions from Ac-228 at 338.4 keV (12%) and Pb-212 at 238.6 keV (44.6%) were used. At UCD, quantification relied solely on the 338.4 keV line, while EPA measurements included several Ac-228 lines (338.4, 911, and 968 keV). UC used the 338.4 keV line for Th-232 activity, verified by the 911 keV line. 

K-40 activity was measured directly from its 1460.8 keV gamma emission, which has a 10.7% emission probability. All full-energy peaks were corrected for potential spectral interferences from overlapping nuclide peaks to ensure accurate results. 

#### _2.5. The Gamma Index (Iγ)_ 

The Gamma Index (Iγ) was calculated as a conservative screening tool to assess the radiological suitability of building materials, following European Commission guidance (European Commission, 2014; CEN, 2022). The index provides a preliminary indication of potential indoor exposure, and when Iγ _>_ 1, a detailed dose assessment is required, accounting for material density, thickness, building type, and intended use bulk or superficial (Markkanen, 1995; European Commission, 1999). The index is defined as: _Iγ_ = ARa _/_ 300 + ATh _/_ 200 + AK _/_ 3000 (2) 

where ARa, ATh, and AK are the activity concentrations of Ra-226, Th232, and K-40 in Bq⋅kg<sup>−1</sup> . The combined standard uncertainty of Iγ was propagated as: u( _Iγ_ ) = (u(A Ra) _/_ 300 )<sup>2</sup> + (u(A Th) _/_ 200 )<sup>2</sup> + (u(A √[ K) _/_ 3000 )<sup>2 ]</sup> 

where u(A_Ra), u(A_Th), and u(A_K) are the standard uncertainties of the respective activity concentrations; uncertainties are reported in Tables 2 and 3. According to the guidelines: 

- Iγ ≤ 1: Material is considered safe for use in bulk applications (e.g., concrete). Note that this criterion applies specifically to bulk building materials. For surface materials (e.g., ceramic tiles), a higher reference level of Iγ = 6 is specified (European Commission, 1999), reflecting their limited contribution to total room exposure. 

- 1 _<_ Iγ ≤ 6: Restricted use may be needed, and detailed dose assessment is recommended. 

- Iγ _>_ 6: Material is not suitable for construction due to high gamma radiation levels. 

While the Gamma Index (Iγ) provides a screening measure for external gamma exposure, it does not account for inhalation of radon (Rn-222) emanating from materials with elevated Ra-226 activity. Radon gas can diffuse from building materials into indoor air and contribute to the overall effective dose, particularly in poorly ventilated spaces. Although the materials analysed in this study generally exhibit low Ra-226 activity, it is noted that higher Ra-226 content can result in increased radon exhalation (Righi and Bruzzi, 2006; El-Taher, 2010 Papaefthymiou and Gouseti, 2008). Therefore, radon exposure should be considered in comprehensive indoor radiological assessments, especially for materials with Ra-226 activity significantly above typical background levels (Turhan, 2008; Fuente et al., 2020).

#### **4. Conclusion
This study provides a comprehensive assessment of naturally occurring radioactive materials (NORM) in building materials commonly used in Ireland, addressing critical data gaps at both national and European levels. Across two experimental phases, 20 representative materials, including demolition-derived aggregates, construction sands, concretes, cementitious additives, recycled industrial by-products, and imported ceramic tiles, were analysed for Ra-226, Th-232, and K-40 activity concentrations using high-resolution gamma spectrometry, with measurements conducted in up to three independent laboratories. 

Most conventional construction materials, including concretes, plaster, sands, and aggregates from low-activity geological regions, exhibited consistently low radionuclide concentrations and Gamma Index (Iγ) values well below the European Commission screening limit of 1. Particle-size fractionation experiments confirmed that grain size has minimal influence on the radiological classification of concrete and similar processed materials, supporting the robustness of standard measurement approaches and confirming their suitability for unrestricted use. 

In contrast, aggregates from high-activity geological formations showed substantially elevated radionuclide concentrations (Ra-226 up to 169 Bq⋅kg<sup>−1</sup> , Th-232 up to 206 Bq⋅kg<sup>−1</sup> , K-40 up to 1155 Bq⋅kg<sup>−1</sup> ), resulting in Gamma Index values approaching 2. This underscores the dominant influence of geological provenance on natural radioactivity levels and highlights the need for quarry-specific radiological screening before large-scale use or blending, in line with European Basic Safety Standards. 

Cementitious additives and secondary materials, such as GGBS and fly ash, exhibited moderate but variable activity concentrations, remaining below regulatory limits for unrestricted use. The detection of trace Cs-137 slightly above the STUK reference level in one fly ash sample demonstrates the importance of targeted assessment for recycled and industrial by-products, whose radiological characteristics may

Imported ceramic tiles showed elevated activity levels that, when assessed against the appropriate surface-material reference level of Iγ = 6 rather than the bulk criterion of Iγ = 1, are well within acceptable limits. This distinction highlights the importance of applying the correct reference value for surface materials and of periodic screening of imported manufactured products. 

Inter-laboratory comparisons revealed high reproducibility, with differences consistent with expected analytical uncertainties, confirming the reliability of gamma spectrometry and supporting harmonised measurement protocols for national and European monitoring of NORM in construction materials. 

From a European perspective, these findings are particularly relevant for sustainable construction, circular economy strategies, and the increasing reuse of demolition and industrial materials. While most materials pose minimal radiological concern, a graded, risk-based monitoring approach is recommended. Materials requiring enhanced investigation include high-activity aggregates, recycled construction and demolition materials, industrial by-products such as fly ash, and imported manufactured products. For these categories, batch-based screening, provenance documentation, and periodic verification can ensure compliance while enabling sustainable material reuse. 

In conclusion, this work expands the national and European dataset on NORM in building materials, provides evidence-based guidance aligned with European regulatory frameworks, and supports the safe integration of low-carbon and recycled materials into the construction sector. The findings contribute to informed material selection, enhanced radiological protection, and the development of harmonised European guidelines that balance safety, sustainability, and environmental responsibility.
