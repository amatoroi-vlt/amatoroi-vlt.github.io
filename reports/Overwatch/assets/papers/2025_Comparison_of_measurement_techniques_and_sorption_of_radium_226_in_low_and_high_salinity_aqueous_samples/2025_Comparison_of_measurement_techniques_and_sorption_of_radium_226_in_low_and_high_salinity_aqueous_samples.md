## Comparison of measurement techniques and sorption of radium-226 in low and high salinity aqueous samples 

Sarah E. Donaher<sup>a,b,*,1</sup> , Jiusi Wang<sup>a</sup> , Nicole E. Martinez<sup>a,c</sup> , Brian A. Powell<sup>a,c</sup>

In addition, rapid and reliable measurements are crucial for time-sensitive samples such as high salinity short-lived isotopes or emergency situations. The objective of this study is to describe the limits of detection, cost, and relative ease for measurement of Ra in both low and high salinity aqueous samples via three analytical methods: liquid scintillation counting (LSC), high purity germanium (HPGe) gamma spectrometry, and inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). To contextualize these measurements for real-world scenarios, the partitioning of<sup>226</sup> Ra to substrates relevant to the marine environment was also characterized. Although HPGe detection with solid phase extraction had the lowest limit of detection for low salinity samples (0.27 Bq L<sup>−1</sup> ), poor<sup>226</sup> Ra recovery for high salinity samples and high materials costs make this method prohibitive for many users. Limits of detection for high salinity samples were lower for LSC (1.28 Bq L<sup>−1</sup> ) than for ICP-MS without dilution (11.4 Bq L<sup>−1</sup> ), but significant and unexpected degradation of the high salinity LSC standards was observed after six months. Therefore, our preferred measurement method for high salinity Ra samples is ICP-MS with sample dilution as necessary to reduce matrix effects. 

### **1. Introduction
Human activities have the potential to relocate and concentrate naturally occurring radioactive material (NORM) such that environmental monitoring or remediation is needed to meet regulatory limits or ensure protection of human health. NORM that has been disturbed or otherwise altered by human activities is often referred to as NORM residues (IAEA, 2013a, 2013b) or technologically enhanced NORM (TENORM) (US EPA, 2008) to distinguish from unimpacted NORM. For example, radium (Ra) is a naturally occurring radionuclide found in elevated activity concentrations (worldwide average of ~1 Bq/g) in phosphogypsum, a waste byproduct of fertilizer production (IAEA, 2013b). Concerns surrounding phosphogypsum stacks, or waste ponds, include the potential for increased radon (Rn) concentrations in the local area as Rn is a gaseous decay product of Ra (IAEA, 2013b). In the United 

States, Rn emissions from phosphogypsum stacks are regulated under the Clean Air Act. NORM residues are also of concern in the oil and gas industry, including Ra in high salinity wastewater (~500–700 Bq L<sup>−1</sup> ) associated with unconventional drilling known as “fracking” (Thakur et al., 2021) and buildup of Ra (~40–1000 Bq/g) in scale of offshore petroleum pipelines (e.g., MacIntosh et al., 2022). The concern with the former is the potential for elevated Rn concentrations and potential for Ra to migrate into the human food chain; the concern with the latter is the potential for direct exposure of marine organisms. 

Exposures to workers, members of the public, and the natural environment from industrial activities involving NORM or NORM residues is not necessarily the dominant risk associated with the activity, and a graded approach to the corresponding management should be integrated and consistent with the management of other hazards (ICRP, 2019). In these cases, robust, sensitive, and practical measurement techniques for environmental samples are needed to ensure that protection standards are being appropriately met, both for human health and environmental protection. Similarly, high quality measurements can inform protection standards by ideally reducing uncertainties. 

Although several recent reviews have described best practices for Ra sample preparation, separation, and measurement in environmental samples (Abbasi, 2018; Boudias et al., 2022; Diego-Feliu et al., 2020; Jia and Jia, 2012; Thakur et al., 2021), the analysis and discussion of these techniques for high salinity samples are less common (but see Henderson et al., 2013; Song et al., 2017; van Beek et al., 2010). Jia and Jia (2012) describe various separation and measurement techniques of Ra in environmental samples and found that alpha (α) spectrometry had a significantly lower minimum detectable activity (MDA) compared to gamma (γ) spectrometry or liquid scintillation counting (LSC), and was therefore a better choice for low-level counting of<sup>226</sup> Ra. Thakur et al. (2021) also reviewed methods of sample preparation for measuring Ra isotopes in environmental samples and note that recent advances in mass spectrometry now allow for measurement of ultra-trace levels of Ra, making it ideal for environmental samples. Although neither of these reviews directly compared low vs high saline water samples, Thakur et al. (2021) note that evaporation (a common pre-concentration technique for measuring Ra in liquid samples) is typically not feasible for seawater samples. 

Typical pre-concentration methods for Ra in both high and low salinity solutions include co-precipitation and adsorption (Thakur et al., 2021). Co-precipitation is also a convenient separations technique and often is done with barium sulfate (BaSO4) as it has a high selectivity for Ra (Jia and Jia, 2012). However, reagent-grade BaSO4 contains trace but variable amounts of<sup>226</sup> Ra, therefore reducing the sensitivity and accuracy of the measurements (Thakur et al., 2021). Adsorption of Ra onto manganese dioxide (MnO2) beads, coated fibers, or cartridges is also a common preconcentration technique. However, this method can be time-consuming, inaccurate, or inefficient (Jia and Jia, 2012). As high salinity matrices can require more intensive pre-concentration techniques (e.g., Song et al., 2017), it is useful to identify measurement methodologies with the lowest limits of detection that also limit the need for high volume samples (e.g., 100–1000s of L) (Henderson et al., 2013) or excessively cumbersome or lengthy sample pre-processing. The high salt content of marine waters typically contributes to a complex sample matrix that is challenging for many analytical approaches. 

This study directly compares rapid turnaround measurements of Ra in low vs high saline water samples via three detection methods characterized by comparatively simple sample preparation and high measurement accuracy: LSC, γ-spectrometry via HPGe, and single quadrupole inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). We describe the detection limits, ease, and cost of each method to help researchers determine which method best suits their samples to minimize sample preparation in situations when rapid turnaround is required. Additionally, because Ra behavior differs between freshwater and marine systems, we conducted batch sorption experiments to calculate the Kd values of<sup>226</sup> Ra to various solids relevant to the marine environment in both high and low salinity solutions. We hypothesized that higher salinity would enhance Ra desorption from sediments and solids in water and result in a lower Kd value, while the lower ionic strength of freshwater solutions would contribute to a greater fraction of bound Ra and a higher Kd value. The results herein are intended to facilitate accurate, rapid, and reliable methods for Ra analysis in high salinity systems which support appropriate assessment and management of environmental exposure to Ra. 

### **2. Materials and methods
### _2.1. Radium-226 standards_ 

Freshwater (0 PSU) and saltwater (35 PSU)<sup>226</sup> Ra standards for the LSC and ICP-MS instruments were created with nominal activity concentrations ranging from 37 to 185,000 Bq kg<sup>−1</sup> (0.001–5 μg kg<sup>−1</sup> ; assuming 1 μg<sup>226</sup> Ra = 1 μCi<sup>226</sup> Ra) in 2 % HNO3. A 3970.1 kBq kg<sup>−1</sup> (107.3 μg kg<sup>−1</sup> )<sup>226</sup> Ra working solution in 2 % HNO3 was first prepared from a 185 kBq mL<sup>−1</sup> (5 μg mL<sup>−1</sup> )<sup>226</sup> Ra stock solution in 1 M HNO3 (Eckert & Ziegler Isotope Products). The exact concentration of<sup>226</sup> Ra in the working solution was confirmed via a HPGe detector (Ortec) with an efficiency calibration based on a NIST traceable<sup>152</sup> Eu source. Freshwater standards were diluted from the stock solution in distilled deionized (DDI) water to a measured mass/activity concentration range of 0.0001–5.4 μg L<sup>−1</sup> (3.7–199,800 Bq L<sup>−1</sup> ). Saltwater standards were diluted from the stock solution in 35 PSU synthetic seawater to a measured mass/activity concentration range of 0.0001–5.3 μg L<sup>−1</sup> (3.7–196,100 Bq L<sup>−1</sup> ). Synthetic seawater at 35 PSU (equivalent to 35 ppt) was created using Instant Ocean® Sea Salt according to the manufacturer’s instructions by adding 42 g of sea salt to 1 L of DDI water. Salinity was checked with a salinity refractometer with automatic temperature compensation with a resolution of 1 ppt (VEE GEE Scientific, LLC). All standards were pH adjusted to ~1 to limit analyte sorption onto container walls using 2 % HNO3. 

### _2.2. Liquid scintillation counting (LSC)_ 

To prepare the standards for liquid scintillation counting (LSC), 10 mL of each standard and 10 mL of ultra-low level liquid scintillation mineral oil (PerkinElmer Ultima Gold™ LLT) was transferred to a glass LSC vial. Using this method, radon-222 (<sup>222</sup> Rn) is extracted from the sample into the mineral oil and the<sup>226</sup> Ra activity is measured indirectly through the activity of the equilibrium progeny, specifically<sup>222</sup> Rn (IAEA, 2010; Jia and Jia, 2012). The primary drawback of this method for environmental samples is the non-specificity for the target analyte as the count will include other naturally occurring radionuclides such as those in the U and Th series (Jia and Jia, 2012). The samples must be sealed and allowed to sit for 30 days for the ingrowth of<sup>222</sup> Rn to reach secular equilibrium with its parent<sup>226</sup> Ra. After 30 days, our sealed standards were vigorously mixed via inversion for 60 s, and left undisturbed for at least 4 h until counting. The standards were counted on two instruments of varying sensitivity, a PerkinElmer Tri-carb 4910 TR and a Quantulus 1220 Ultra. Additional instrument and counting details are available in the Supporting Information. After counting, standards were allowed to sit on a lab bench for six months and then were recounted on the PerkinElmer Tri-carb 4910 TR to detect any difference in quench between the two sets of standards over time.

### _2.4. Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS)_ 

Two approaches were considered for determination of detection limits for<sup>226</sup> Ra in liquid samples using high resolution, high purity germanium (HPGe) detectors: (1) direct measurement of 250 mL of solution and (2) solid phase extraction of 1000 mL of solution onto specialized Solid Phase Extraction (SPE) filter disks (CDS Analytical 3291 Empore™ Radium SPE RAD Disk). In both cases a NIST traceable 152Eu source in 0.502 mL of 2 % nitric acid (Eckert and Zeigler Isotope Products, Atlanta, GA) was used to determine HPGe efficiency curves. A<sup>152</sup> Eu standard was used due to the lack of availability of a similarly traceable<sup>226</sup> Ra standard. A Canberra GC 4018 HPGe cooled using a Cryo-cycle interfacing with a Lynx multichannel analyzer and Prospect software was used for all measurements. 

In the first direct measurement scenario, a 250 mL DDI water sample was spiked with 252 Bq (6811 pCi) of the<sup>152</sup> Eu source and then measured directly for 24 h. The background count rate was determined by taking a 24-h measurement with no sample within the detector. Measurement data was used for energy calibration and determination of detector efficiency (Figure S1A). The<sup>226</sup> Ra peak ROI of 184.7–187.8 keV was used to define the background region of the<sup>152</sup> Eu spectra and the LLD and MDC for<sup>226</sup> Ra were calculated as described for the LSC method. Defining the background region as that of the<sup>226</sup> Ra gamma of interest enables determination of LLD and MDC. 

Note that this measurement was only completed for freshwater but would be consistent with that in saltwater. Although photointeractions are influenced by material density and composition, 186 keV photons are of high enough energy to be unimpacted by the subtle differences associated with level of salinity, particularly at the spatial scale of interest. For example, total attenuation for water with no salt compared to water with 35 ‰ salt (i.e., NaCl) for 186 keV photons is 0.1404 cm<sup>2</sup> /g compared to 0.1400 cm<sup>2</sup> /g, respectively (NIST XCOM). 

To determine detector efficiency for the SPE filter disk scenario, a 12.64 Bq (341.6 pCi) aliquot of<sup>152</sup> Eu solution was added to a glass fiber filter adhered with tape to a piece of clear packaging tape. The packaging tape was included to prevent any of the solution from going onto the bench or absorbent paper while the sample was drying. After drying overnight, a second filter paper was placed on top of the<sup>152</sup> Eu amended filter and both were sealed with an additional layer of packing tape. The assembled filters were then placed in a 2” diameter Petri dish which was then counted for 24 h on the HPGe. 

The<sup>226</sup> Ra recovery for low ionic strength solutions was assumed to be quantitative (≥95 %) during SPE filtration according to manufacturer’s instructions, but typical saltwater ion concentrations exceeded the recommended levels (Table S1; 3 M, 2000). Therefore, to determine 226Ra recovery for typical seawater applications, two filtration replicates were extracted according to the manufacturer’s instructions (3 M, 2000). Briefly, 225 Bq (6.08 nCi) of<sup>226</sup> Ra was added from a 3970.1 kBq kg<sup>−1</sup> (107.3 μg kg<sup>−1</sup> )<sup>226</sup> Ra working solution in 2 % HNO3 to 1 L of 35 PSU synthetic seawater and the sample was acidified to 1 M with concentrated HNO3. An Empore™ SPE disk was carefully placed on a vacuum filtration system which was maintained at an approximate flow rate of 50 mL/min throughout the rest of the process. The SPE disk was first washed with approximately 20 mL of 2 M HNO3 and then the sample was immediately added. After the entire sample was filtered, the SPE disk was once again rinsed with approximately 20 mL of 2 M HNO3 followed by an additional drying rinse of 20 mL of acetone. The disk was then dried at 60<sup>◦</sup> C on a metal planchet for 30 min before being sealed in a 55 mm × 60 mm mylar bag. The bag was directly placed onto the shielded HPGe detector and counted for 24 h. ### _2.5. Batch sorption experiment_ 

Batch sorption experiments were conducted to calculate the Kd values of<sup>226</sup> Ra to various solids in both artificial (Instant Ocean® Sea Salt) 600 mM saltwater and 10 mM NaCl solutions. Four solids were chosen as adsorbents: quartz, crushed _Geukensia demissa_ (Atlantic ribbed mussel) shell primarily consisting of calcite, a montmorillonite clay, and iron oxide (goethite). Either 0.25 g (quartz, crushed shell, and montmorillonite) or 0.05 g (goethite) of the adsorbent was added to a preweighed 15 mL centrifuge tube and then reweighed. ### **3. Results and discussion
### _3.1. Liquid scintillation counting_ 

The Tri-carb and Quantulus LSC instruments both recorded higher counts for the saltwater standards than freshwater standards of the same 226Ra concentration (Fig. 1A). The detection limit for LSC is a function of counting time, sample volume, and instrument sensitivity, with previous studies finding detection limits around 0.05–20 Bq L<sup>−1</sup> (1.35–540.4 pCi L<sup>−1</sup> ) for Ra (Jia and Jia, 2012). Our results are in good agreement, with detection limits ranging from 0.07 to 1.28 Bq L<sup>−1</sup> (1.89–34.6 pCi L<sup>−1</sup> ) for one-month old samples (Tables 2 and 3). The MDC for the saltwater standards was marginally higher than the freshwater standards for both instruments, although at higher concentrations a greater difference between counts was observed compared to lower concentrations (Table 2; Fig. 1A). The Tri-carb instrument MDC was ~13× greater than the Quantulus instrument for both fresh- and saltwater standards, indicating lower sensitivity by approximately an order of magnitude (Table 2). Ultimately, the salinity did not greatly impact the detection of<sup>226</sup> Ra in freshly made samples via LSC. However, for the six-month old standards, we observed a minor increase (28 %) in the MDC for the freshwater standards (i.e., only a small increase in quench) but the MDC for the saltwater standards more than doubled, from 1.28 Bq L<sup>−1</sup> to 2.71 Bq L<sup>−1</sup> (1.89–73.2 pCi L<sup>−1</sup> ) (Table 4; Fig. 1B). This was attributed to significant change in the coloration of the saltwater standards over six months (Fig. 2). 

In LSC, the sample is combined with solvent and a scintillator (“fluor”) to stimulate production of photons which are quantified to determine the sample activity. This method is ideal for low energy beta- 

and alpha-emitters. The emitted energy excites solvent molecules and is then transferred to the scintillator which produces photons detected by a photoemitter tube (PMT). The primary advantages of this technique are the relative ease of sample preparation, ability to measure high sample volume, and high counting efficiency up to 100 % (Knoll, 2010). The drawbacks include large instrument sizes, poor resolution contributing to difficulties in the identification of radionuclides, reductions in efficiency due to chemical or color quenching, and the hazardous nature of some of the chemicals used for the fluor component (Knoll, 2010). Commonly, LSC measurement of<sup>226</sup> Ra activity is quantified from the decay products, so the long wait time to reach secular equilibrium (~30 days) in the sealed sample is an additional drawback specific to this radionuclide. In this study, the interlinked consequences associated with this decay time and the associated color quenching were significantly impactful. 

To the best of our knowledge, the significant sample discoloration observed in this study has not been previously reported but represent a significant limitation for this method with saltwater samples. Although the mechanisms for this color change are currently unknown, the severity of the reaction over time is variable among samples but appears to progress over the course of multiple years in samples with salinity ≥20 PSU (Figure S2). Defining the underlying mechanisms behind this degradation and identifying how to either stop or slow the associated color quenching represents an interesting area for future study. Prior studies have noted variations in quench resistance among LSC cocktails in samples stored on timescales of months to years (Feng et al., 2015), indicating that thoughtful selection of cocktail is critical for samples that either require an ingrowth period or long storage time. The LSC cocktail used in this study, Ultima Gold™ LLT, has been shown to be an average to above average performer when compared among nine commercially-available LSC cocktails in terms of<sup>3</sup> H/<sup>14</sup> C counting efficiency, quench resistance, and sample stability, although some evidence suggested that salinity decreased this performance (Verrezen et al., 2008). However, there are generally few direct comparisons available between low and high salinity samples measured via LSC, although some studies have reported increased quenching in high salinity samples (Palomo et al., 2011). 

A Ra-specific LSC cocktail RADAEX® has previously been developed which contains 2-methyl-2-2-heptylnonanoic acid (HMHN) and dicyclohaxano-21-crown-7 (Cy221Cy) as the solvent extractants (McDowell et al., 1989). High salinity environmental samples are vulnerable to a variety of mechanisms for quenching beyond the discoloration observed in this study, such as the aforementioned precipitation of salt or presence of other suspended solids which can cause physical quenching, or chemical quenching caused by certain molecules present in seawater such as dissolved oxygen or organic acids (Schubert and Kallmeyer, 2023). For the discolored high salinity samples in this study, potential but unverified mitigation strategies include decoloration via ultraviolet radiation (UV) exposure or addition of a small volume of 30 % hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) to bleach the sample (US NRC, 2011). Note that opening the samples to add H2O2 would require waiting for the 30-day ingrowth period again prior to measuring. Although the aim of this study was to compare detection methods characterized by comparatively simple sample preparation, future studies should explore if additional sample pre-treatment such as filtration or addition of a pH buffer reduces the observed discoloration in the samples. Therefore, LSC may be a convenient method for Ra detection in environmental saltwater samples, but the user must remain cognizant of the impacts to measurement from possible discoloration or degradation of the solvent or fluor components over time.

HPGe detectors, a type of semiconductor detector, are used for a variety of applications including environmental monitoring, nuclear forensics, and threat reduction (Casagrande and Bonotto, 2018; US EPA, 2019; Rameback et al., 2023¨ ). As with LSC, we were unable to identify any prior studies reporting direct comparisons between low and high salinity samples, but HPGe has effectively been used to determine activity in high salinity samples (Grabowski and Bem, 2010) and even for _in situ_ underwater measurements in seawater (Povinec et al., 1996). However, both methods require a large sample volume and relatively long counting time, which may make HPGe detection infeasible in cases of low sample amounts and/or high numbers of independent samples. 

Note that another concern when measuring<sup>226</sup> Ra in seawater with HPGe is the potential interference of natural uranium, which has a concentration in 35 ‰ seawater of about 3.3 μg/L (Ku et al., 1977). Although our synthetic seawater standards did not contain uranium, uranium-235 (0.72 % of naturally occurring uranium) emits a gamma of 185 keV in its decay (ICRP, 2008) and therefore interferes with the ability to clearly distinguish the<sup>226</sup> Ra peak in the gamma spectrum. 226Ra and its progeny have additional gammas that can be measured to disambiguate from<sup>235</sup> U (e.g., the gamma emission of the progeny isotope<sup>214</sup> Bi at 1764 keV), but samples would need to meet the conditions for being in secular equilibrium to do so. 

We also report here an unsatisfactory<sup>226</sup> Ra recovery yield of _<_ 5 % for the SPE disks, likely due to the large seawater sample volume measured (1 L). Lagac´e et al. (2017) also noted a relationship between sample volume and Ra recovery from high salinity samples onto a strong cation exchange resin (AG50W-X8), with recoveries decreasing from 99 ± 4 % for 10 mL samples of natural seawater to 57 ± 8 % for 90 mL samples. This was attributed to increased mass of salts loaded onto the resin. The recovery of<sup>226</sup> Ra in high salinity fracking wastewater for the Empore™ SPE disks has also previously been reported at 13 ± 1 % (Nelson et al., 2014). Taken together with our results, the previously reported low recoveries for high salinity environmental samples suggest that the use of SPE disks is extremely limited for high ionic strength solutions.

An increase in salinity is typically associated with a decrease in total metal sorption capacity to various solids (Acosta et al., 2011; Esfandiar et al., 2022). This is broadly attributed to higher salinity promoting the desorption due to the presence of competing ions. This competition is expected to be particularly influential for divalent cations such as Ca<sup>2+</sup> and Mg<sup>2+</sup> , both of which are prevalent in seawater. However, some studies do report increasing sorption with increased salinity (Malamis and Katsou, 2013), as we observed for the quartz solid (Fig. 4), suggesting the potential for more complex underlying mechanisms. Ultimately, these results provide insight into the behavior of Ra in complex estuarine environments characterized by the presence of multiple mineral and solid forms. 

### **4. Conclusions
Three analytical approaches were compared for rapid turnaround measurements of<sup>226</sup> Ra in simulated freshwater and seawater samples. HPGe detection following Ra extraction on an Empore™ Solid Phase Extraction (SPE) Disk had the lowest limit of detection for freshwater samples but is also the costliest measurement technique. Furthermore, there is a significant penalty to total recovery and therefore increased limits of detection for high salinity samples due to competition for sorption sites on the extraction disk with other dissolved ions. HPGe measurement of Ra also requires a greater sample volume than measurement versus LSC or ICP-MS, which represents a meaningful limitation for this method. Direct measurement of<sup>226</sup> Ra using quadrupole ICPMS provided the second lowest detection limit for the low salinity samples. The drawbacks to ICP-MS include significant sample matrix effects as demonstrated by the suppressed sensitivity for the high salinity samples and the high cost of a traceable<sup>226</sup> Ra standard to produce the necessary calibration curve which may be prohibitive. 

LSC presented the simplest method and had the lowest detection limit for high salinity samples. However, the method requires a 28-day waiting period for progeny ingrowth and the present study demonstrates that the seawater matrix has the potential to alter the consistency and coloration of scintillation fluid, which caused increased quenching and decreased detection efficiency after six months. The continuous but unpredictable degradation of both the Ra LSC standards and samples make it challenging to standardize the overall impact to detection efficiency, necessitating the frequent creation of new sets of standards (including waiting for the ingrowth period), which can be timeconsuming, cumbersome, and potentially expensive. Thus, the recommended approach for accurate rapid turnaround measurements of Ra in marine waters is ICP-MS with situation-specific dilution to reduce the matrix effects. LSC may remain the preferred technique in cases of costor expertise-constraints, or when preservation of the samples beyond the initial ingrowth period is not needed.
